The project

Overview

A wearable robot needs more than a single estimate of where the user is in the gait cycle. It must also distinguish purposeful walking from fidgeting, anticipate a change in terrain, and interpret movement in its current context. Tread connects these quantities using a chest-mounted RGB camera and four lower-limb IMUs, with inference deployed onboard over ROS 2 and micro-ROS.

The architecture is causal: visual terrain perception selects the relevant locomotion expert, whose posterior is retained as a probabilistic context for a shared gait encoder. Phase and speed decoders then reuse that representation. The study evaluates perception and estimation, with qualitative indoor and outdoor demonstrations; closed-loop assistance driven by Tread remains future work.

My contributions

  • Developed the terrain-conditioned visuo-inertial inference chain and its wearable ROS 2/micro-ROS implementation.
  • Connected transfer-learned terrain perception to inertial locomotion experts with explicit fidget rejection.
  • Retained the locomotion posterior as probabilistic context for a shared LSTM representation and bilateral phase and speed decoders.
  • Evaluated unseen-user estimation and terrain transitions while preserving the boundaries of separately collected training protocols.

01

Synchronized sensing at the edge

A body-worn inference platform

A chest-mounted smartphone supplies RGB images of the upcoming walking surface. Four BNO085 IMUs on the lower limbs provide sensor-fused orientation, acquired through ESP32 microcontrollers and micro-ROS. A Jetson AGX Orin carries out the inference, with computation and battery housed in a wearable backpack.

The inertial pathway uses pitch and yaw orientation features rather than raw accelerometer and gyroscope channels. Pitch describes sagittal motion relative to gravity, while within-window yaw changes capture turning without relying on absolute heading. This feature choice reduces sensitivity to differences in sensor mounting and connects the same wearable signals to locomotion, phase, and speed estimation.

02

Anticipatory Terrain Perception

Seeing the transition before stepping onto it

The Anticipatory Terrain Perception module classifies level ground, ascending stairs, and descending stairs from egocentric RGB frames. A frozen ResNet-18 backbone supplies visual features to a trainable classifier head. Dataset construction combines ExoNet images with custom frames and uses a terrain-dominance criterion to label what the camera sees.

Visual context is useful because it can change before the wearer’s leg motion does. ATP achieves 96.3% held-out terrain-classification accuracy and anticipates transitions by 1.46 s on average, with substantial variation across transitions. Its purpose in the pipeline is to select an appropriate locomotion expert; the demonstrated terrain vocabulary is limited to level ground and stairs.

03

Terrain-Aware Locomotion Mode Identification

Locomotion experts with fidget rejection

Terrain selects between two LSTM locomotion experts. The level-ground expert distinguishes standing, fidgeting, level walking, and left or right turning; the stair expert distinguishes level walking, stair ascent, and stair descent. Temporal statistics of pitch and yaw changes summarize short IMU windows before temporal stacking and classification.

The hierarchy lets datasets collected under separate level-ground and stair protocols contribute to one inference chain without treating every activity as if it had been recorded together. Crucially, the selected expert’s soft probabilities are retained. This Probabilistic Locomotion Context carries uncertainty and mixed evidence forward rather than reducing every downstream decision to an abrupt hard mode switch.

04

Shared Gait Kinematic Encoder

One gait representation, two estimation tasks

A 64-unit LSTM encoder combines kinematic information with the locomotion probability vector. The phase decoder predicts bilateral sine–cosine phase coordinates, supporting continuous cyclic phase estimates at 50 Hz and avoiding a discontinuity at the end of each gait cycle. Context helps the shared representation distinguish level walking from ascent and descent.

After training for phase estimation, the encoder is frozen and its latent sequence is reused by a convolutional speed decoder operating at 2 Hz. This transfer of a learned gait representation connects the two tasks without requiring separate terrain-specific phase networks. The reported speed evaluation covers treadmill-labelled level walking from 3.5 to 5.5 km/h.

05

Unseen-user evaluation and demonstrations

Continuous inference across changing context

Leave-one-subject-out evaluation yields gait-phase RMSEs of 1.75%, 2.97%, and 4.55% for level walking, stair ascent, and stair descent. The same evaluation strategy gives a walking-speed RMSE of 0.218 ± 0.072 km/h within the treadmill-labelled range. Transition analyses complement aggregate accuracy by examining how quickly the pipeline responds when terrain or locomotion changes.

A supplementary demonstration presents onboard operation in indoor and outdoor settings. The indoor layout combines terrain perception, locomotion mode and bilateral phase; the outdoor layout combines mode, phase and estimated speed. The numerical speed result remains tied to its treadmill reference. Earlier smart-insole work provides a methodological predecessor based on FSR timing and anthropometry, with a different sensing and estimation approach.
