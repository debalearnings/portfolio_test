The project

Overview

How can a wearable robot communicate a direction through a movement the wearer already understands? XoNav investigates this question by using the hip as a kinesthetic navigation interface. Four quasi-direct-drive actuators render Advance, two levels of Braking, and two levels of left or right Steering across the sagittal and frontal planes. Rather than continuously imposing a trajectory, the controller coordinates cues with wearer-initiated walking and suppresses them during standing.

The system brings together adjustable wearable hardware, joint impedance control, IMU-based gait-state inference, and constrained Bayesian personalization. Blindfolded Wizard-of-Oz experiments evaluate the cue interface separately from a subsequent camera-based autonomous-navigation feasibility study. The current evidence comes from sighted participants in controlled indoor environments; evaluation with people with visual impairment is a future step.

My contributions

  • Developed and integrated the four-DoF hip interface, embedded communication, and joint-impedance cue-rendering pipeline within the CMERI research team.
  • Implemented bilateral gait-state inference from two thigh IMUs to synchronize cues with walking and suppress cue delivery during standing.
  • Developed detectability-constrained and preference-based Bayesian personalization for graded Braking and Steering cues.
  • Integrated the haptic interface with RTAB-Map and Nav2 and evaluated both operator-guided and autonomous indoor navigation.

01

Hardware and impedance control

A wearable interface at the hip

Each hip has flexion–extension and abduction–adduction actuation, allowing sagittal resistance and frontal steering to be rendered through distinct joints. Adjustable waist width and thigh height accommodate the wearer, while a backpack houses computation and power. A Jetson Orin Nano communicates with AK80-9 quasi-direct-drive motors through MD80 drivers; a chest-mounted RGB-D camera supports the navigation experiments.

High-level commands specify the impedance behavior to be felt at the joint, with torque and current control handled at the motor driver. This division connects a comparatively low-impedance physical interface to interpretable movement cues, while preserving separate layers for navigation, gait synchronization, and actuation.

02

Advance · Braking · Steering

A vocabulary of gait-informed cues

Advance encourages continued progression through sagittal-plane actuation. Braking creates resistance to forward thigh swing, with Slow-down and Stop levels that differ in stiffness. Steering uses the frontal-plane actuators to provide left or right directional guidance, with Gentle and Sharp levels. The cues therefore convey both an action and its urgency through the mechanical behavior of the interface.

Steering follows a trapezoidal position profile whose rise, hold, and return occupy 30%, 40%, and 30% of its duration. It begins during stance on the turning side; a command received during swing waits for that side’s next heel strike. This timing rule links the requested turn to a suitable support phase rather than interrupting the leg arbitrarily.

03

Subject-independent gait inference

Timing guidance from two thigh IMUs

A bilateral LSTM classifier identifies standing and the stance or swing state of each leg from two thigh-mounted IMUs. The deployed estimator runs at 20 Hz, providing the discrete state information needed by the cue scheduler. Heel-contact measurements are used to establish reference labels during model development, rather than becoming additional sensing requirements for the deployed classifier.

Leave-one-subject-out evaluation examines whether the model transfers to an unseen wearer. Reported class recalls span 95.2–97.9%; mean transition delays before the two-prediction confirmation step are 38.6 ms for stance-to-swing and 63.1 ms for swing-to-stance. The role of this model is cue coordination; it is distinct from Tread’s continuous, terrain-conditioned gait-phase estimator.

04

Constrained human-in-the-loop optimization

Detectable cues that remain comfortable

Personalization treats cue recognition as a constraint rather than assuming that a stronger cue is always better. Each candidate is assessed through 15 randomized scored presentations and must achieve at least 13 correct responses. Braking optimization minimizes the mean stiffness of its two levels while analytically screening candidates against a maximum Stop-stiffness bound before presentation.

Steering optimization adjusts the Sharp cue’s additional amplitude and duration while keeping the Gentle amplitude fixed. Pairwise comparisons with the incumbent capture the wearer’s comfort preference, and the same recognition requirement preserves distinguishability. The two objectives deliberately differ: low resistance for Braking and individual comfort for Steering.

05

Human experiments and navigation integration

From a cue interface to indoor wayfinding

In the Wizard-of-Oz study, ten sighted participants navigated blindfolded along U-, N-, and figure-eight-shaped paths while an operator selected cues. Across 150 trials, 98.8% of walked distance remained within a 0.75 m passage. Separating cue selection from interface use makes this study evidence for the personalized guidance channel without attributing its performance to autonomous perception.

A separate feasibility study connects the body-worn camera to RTAB-Map and Nav2, translating navigation commands into the same cue vocabulary. Fourteen of 15 nominal trials and eight of nine trials with an unmapped obstacle reached the goal. Contacts and failure cases remain part of the reported study, so these demonstrations establish indoor feasibility rather than independent mobility in unrestricted environments.
