XoNav: directions you can feel

A four-actuator hip exoskeleton that turns navigation commands into gait-timed pushes, brakes and turns, personalized for each wearer and tested with people walking blindfolded.

The idea

Walking without vision needs guidance that is quick to read and hard to miss. Audio and vibration cues have to be translated into movement; XoNav renders the instruction where the movement happens, as small torques at the hips timed to the wearer’s own steps.

The project joins hardware, gait inference, constrained personalization and navigation into one physical interface, and tests it first with an operator choosing cues and then with a planner.

  • Built the bilateral LSTM gait-state estimator and the detectability-constrained, preference-based Bayesian personalization.

01Hardware and impedance control

Four motors, two planes, one wearable

Each hip carries a sagittal flexion–extension actuator and a frontal abduction–adduction actuator. They are quasi-direct-drive units, so they stay back-drivable and almost unnoticeable until a cue is played. Waist width and thigh height adjust to the wearer.

A backpack holds the 6S Li-ion pack, the drivers and a Jetson Orin Nano running ROS 2; a chest-mounted RealSense D435i looks ahead. The Jetson sends impedance targets and the drivers close the torque and current loops, so a cue feels like a spring rather than a shove.

On the wearer

Built to be worn outdoors

Hip modules, waist belt and backpack sit close to the body, so the wearer walks normally until a cue arrives.

02Cue design

A vocabulary written in torque

XoNav speaks four words. Advance nudges the swinging leg forward through the sagittal actuators. Braking adds stance resistance in two grades, Slow-down and Stop. Steer Left and Steer Right move the thigh in the frontal plane, in two grades, Gentle and Sharp.

Timing matters as much as shape. A steering cue follows a trapezoidal profile (30% rise, 40% hold, 30% return) that starts in stance on the turning side; a command that arrives mid-swing waits for the next heel strike instead of interrupting the step.

03Gait-state inference

Knowing when to push

A bilateral LSTM reads only the two thigh IMUs and labels each leg as standing, stance or swing. Cues are scheduled against these states and muted while the wearer stands still.

  • 95.2–97.9% recall on held-out participants (leave-one-subject-out)
  • Mean detection delay 38.6 ms for stance-to-swing and 63.1 ms for swing-to-stance

04Constrained human-in-the-loop optimization

Firm enough to notice, gentle enough to wear

A stronger cue is not automatically a better one. Each wearer’s Braking and Steering cues are tuned by Bayesian optimization in which detectability is a constraint, not a hope: a candidate first passes an analytic safety screen, is then played in randomized trials, and counts only if the wearer names it correctly in at least 13 of 15 presentations.

Braking is tuned for the least resistance that still reads clearly; Steering for comfort, through pairwise comparisons against the current best. The firmest personalized Stop stayed below 53% of the safety limit.

05Human study

U, N and 8, with eyes covered

Ten sighted participants walked blindfolded along U-, N- and 8-shaped corridors while an operator outside the path chose the cues (a Wizard-of-Oz setup). This separates the question “can people follow the hip?” from “can the robot plan?”.

  • 150 guided trials; 98.8% of the walked distance stayed inside a 0.75 m passage
  • Progression speed rose 4.9% per trial (0.24 → 0.29 m/s) while wander, heading error and corrective reversals fell
  • Every participant could tell apart the two grades of their own cues

From U1

06Perception and planning

Handing the route to the map

The same cue vocabulary was then driven by a planner. The chest D435i, in stereo-inertial mode, builds an RTAB-Map of the arena; Nav2 plans the route and its velocity commands become Advance, Steer and Slow-down cues.

  • 14 of 15 nominal runs reached the goal
  • 8 of 9 runs followed a re-planned detour around an obstacle missing from the map

A feasibility study with blindfolded sighted participants in a furnished indoor arena; studies with people with visual impairment are future work.
